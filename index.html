AI for Research and Manufacturing

AI meets manufacturing | AI meets biology

I work at the intersection of artificial intelligence, research, development, and manufacturing. I am Digitalization Manager at BASF, an Adjunct Professor in Chemical Engineering and a Visiting Scientist in Biological Sciences at LSU. For 25+ years, I have built tools that put data to work in industry, in academia and at a software startup. I speak biology, engineering and computer science. I take bioinformatics and industrial data systems from the first requirement to deployment.

AI meets manufacturing

I have helped lead digitalization at BASF, and now its work in AI. In research and development at BASF Plant Science in Research Triangle Park, North Carolina, I helped transform plant biotechnology research for the next-generation sequencing era. I led the development of pipelines that annotate and characterize plant genes and genomes, and I helped identify genes involved in drought tolerance and other important crop traits. I then moved to BASF's site in Geismar, Louisiana, the company's largest manufacturing site in North America. There I have worked in knowledge management, digitalization, data science and analytics, and AI. I have built dashboards and decision-support tools for raw material planning, production planning and process optimization. I work with cross-functional teams to turn business needs into tools for research and manufacturing.

With colleagues in Chemical Engineering at LSU and at BASF, we use machine learning to model chemical production. We predict quality and production variables that cannot be measured directly (soft sensors), detect anomalies in equipment sensor data, and use unsupervised clustering to approximate manufacturing operating conditions. We recently described a graph-temporal surrogate architecture for multivariate process modeling (Pereira et al. 2027).

AI meets biology

I build pipelines for evolutionary biology and apply them to gene families across the tree of life. For each family, I compare the genes from many species to see how they are related and what their ancestors looked like. I then look for unusual patterns of change and check whether those changes are likely to affect how the protein works. With colleagues in Biological Sciences at LSU, we are studying gene families involved in protein quality control, lysosome and mitochondrial function, and healthy aging (Pandey et al. 2025; Ricaurte-Perez et al. 2025).

My research on gene families began in plants, where I studied how genes and genomes evolved as flowering plants diversified. At Penn State, I developed pipelines for genome annotation and analysis, and I helped annotate and publish the poplar and papaya genomes (Tuskan et al. 2006; Ming et al. 2008). I built PlantTribes, a database of plant gene families (Wall et al. 2008). My other work includes the MADS-box and APETALA2-like transcription factors in flower development (Kim et al. 2006; Shan et al. 2009), and microRNAs (Barakat et al. 2007a, 2007b). I also studied plant genome duplication: whole-genome duplications and polyploidy in the history of flowering plants and their link to diversification (Cui et al. 2006; Soltis et al. 2009), the fate of duplicated genes (Duarte et al. 2006), and genes that plant genomes share as single copies (Duarte et al. 2010).
