Research

I work at the intersection of artificial intelligence, research, development, and manufacturing. I am Digitalization Manager at BASF, an Adjunct Professor in Chemical Engineering and a Visiting Scientist in Biological Sciences at LSU.

I have spent 25+ years turning data into tools that people use: in industry at BASF, in academic genomics at Penn State and LSU, and at a software startup. My background combines biology, engineering and computer science, and I have used all three throughout my career.

I design and build bioinformatics and industrial data systems, from requirements through deployment, and I choose open-source software when it fits. I mentor development teams on best practice and lead cross-functional teams.

AI meets evolutionary biology

I build pipelines for evolutionary biology and apply them to gene families across the tree of life. For each family, I compare the genes from many species to see how they are related and what their ancestors looked like. I then look for unusual patterns of change and check whether those changes are likely to affect how the protein works. With colleagues in Biological Sciences at LSU, we are studying gene families involved in protein quality control, lysosome and mitochondrial function, and healthy aging (Pandey et al. 2025; Ricaurte-Perez et al. 2025).

My earlier published work on gene families focuses on plants. At Penn State, I developed pipelines for genome annotation and analysis, and I helped annotate and publish the poplar and papaya genomes (Tuskan et al. 2006; Ming et al. 2008). I built PlantTribes, a database of plant gene families (Wall et al. 2008). My other work includes the MADS-box and APETALA2-like transcription factors in flower development (Kim et al. 2006; Shan et al. 2009), and microRNAs (Barakat et al. 2007a, 2007b). I also studied plant genome duplication: whole-genome duplications and polyploidy in the history of flowering plants and their link to diversification (Cui et al. 2006; Soltis et al. 2009), the fate of duplicated genes (Duarte et al. 2006), and genes that plant genomes share as single copies (Duarte et al. 2010).

AI meets manufacturing

I have helped lead BASF into both the digital age and now the AI age. In research and development at BASF Plant Science in Research Triangle Park, North Carolina, I helped transform plant biotechnology research for the next-generation sequencing era. I led the development of pipelines that annotate and characterize plant genes and genomes, and I helped identify genes involved in drought tolerance and other important crop traits. I then moved to BASF's site in Geismar, Louisiana, the company's largest manufacturing site in North America. There I've worked in knowledge management, digitalization, data science and analytics, and AI. I work with cross-functional teams to turn business needs into tools for research and manufacturing.

With colleagues in Chemical Engineering at LSU and at BASF, we use machine learning to model chemical production. We predict quality and production variables that cannot be measured directly (soft sensors), detect anomalies in equipment sensor data, build dynamic dashboards for quality and production data, and use unsupervised clustering to approximate manufacturing operating conditions. We recently described a graph-temporal surrogate architecture for multivariate process modeling (Pereira et al. 2026).
